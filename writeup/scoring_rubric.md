# Scoring Rubric

## Research quality (30%)

### Categories

* Methodology & approach: Choosing appropriate methods for the research question determines how reliable and informative the results will be. We evaluate how well projects match their methodology to their questions and justify their approach.  
* Literature integration: Science usually advances by building systematically on existing work rather than starting from scratch. We evaluate how meaningfully projects engage with and build on the relevant literature.  
* Project scope & focus: Most research projects fail due to poor scoping rather than poor execution. Focusing on the most important elements enables meaningful progress on the core question instead of covering many areas with little depth. We evaluate whether projects have a well-defined and strategically chosen scope.

### Scoring rubric

| Score | Methodology & approach | Literature integration | Project scope & focus |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1 | No clear methodology or completely inappropriate approach with no justification  | Ignores existing work entirely or shows no awareness of relevant literature | Scope is either trivial (could be completed in a few days) or wildly unrealistic (would require at least months of work) |
| 2-3 | Basic methodology with poor fit for research question; minimal or weak justification  | Limited engagement with existing work; superficial citations that don't actually inform the approach  | Scope is somewhat problematic – either too narrow to be meaningful or overly ambitious with no strategic focusing |
| 4-5 | Reasonable methodology that mostly fits the research question; some justification provided | Some engagement with relevant literature but misses key sources or fails to meaningfully build on existing work | Scope is adequate but shows limited evidence of strategic thinking about what to prioritize |
| 6-7 | Good methodology choices that fit the research question well; clear justification for approach | Good engagement with relevant literature; builds meaningfully on existing work in most areas where appropriate | Well-scoped project that is ambitious but achievable; some evidence of strategic focusing |
| 8-9 | Strong methodology that is well-suited to research question; thoughtful justification that considers alternatives | Comprehensive engagement with relevant literature; clearly builds on and extends existing work where appropriate | Excellent scope management – ambitious yet realistic with clear evidence of strategic prioritization |
| 10 | Exceptional methodology that perfectly fits research question; sophisticated justification considering multiple approaches and trade-offs | Masterful integration of existing literature; advances the field in a meaningful way while building systematically on prior work where appropriate | Perfect scope balance – maximally ambitious while remaining achievable; sophisticated strategic thinking about priorities |

## Project importance (30%)

### Categories

* Problem importance: Time and resources are limited, so research should focus on problems where progress would make the biggest difference. We evaluate whether projects make a compelling case for why their chosen problem deserves attention over alternatives.  
* Approach & innovation: Effective research requires choosing promising approaches from among many possible solutions. We evaluate whether projects identify and justify particularly promising angles for making progress. Novel or innovative approaches receive bonus consideration.  
* Theory of change: Research without clear pathways to application often sits unused. We evaluate whether projects articulate realistic mechanisms for influencing decisions, policies, or practices.

### Scoring rubric

| Score | Problem importance | Approach & innovation | Theory of change |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1 | No arguments provided for problem importance or clearly unimportant issue | No justification for why this approach matters; arbitrary or illogical angle; no innovation or poorly conceived novelty | No coherent theory of change; completely disconnected from any plausible impact pathway |
| 2-3 | Weak arguments for problem importance; may be somewhat important but poorly justified | Limited justification for approach; unclear why this angle matters for solving the problem; minimal innovation or standard approach with slight variations | Vague or mostly incoherent theory of change; major gaps in impact logic |
| 4-5 | Some reasonable arguments for problem importance but not fully convincing | Some justification for approach but doesn't clearly connect to problem solution; some novel elements but not well-integrated or justified | Basic theory of change with some logical gaps; partially plausible impact pathway |
| 6-7 | Good arguments for problem importance; convincing case that this matters | Clear justification for why this approach/angle is promising for addressing the problem; meaningful innovation that adds value or solid execution of established methods | Mostly coherent theory of change; reasonable impact pathway with minor gaps |
| 8-9 | Strong arguments for problem importance; compelling case for why this is a priority issue | Excellent justification for approach; clear connection between angle and potential solution; significant innovation that is thoughtfully integrated, or exceptional execution of proven approaches | Strong theory of change; clear, logical pathway from research to impact |
| 10 | Exceptional arguments for problem importance; masterful case for why this is among the most critical issues | Perfect justification; approach is clearly optimal or near-optimal for addressing this problem; groundbreaking innovation that opens new research directions, or masterful application of existing methods | Exceptional theory of change; sophisticated understanding of impact pathways and implementation |

## Research integrity (25%)

### Categories

* Research & reasoning transparency: Hidden failures and unstated assumptions make research misleading and impossible to replicate. We evaluate whether projects honestly acknowledge what didn't work, state key assumptions clearly, and are transparent about data constraints and methodological limitations.  
* Alternative perspectives: Researchers naturally become attached to their hypotheses and interpretations, risking confirmation bias. We evaluate whether projects actively consider objections and alternative explanations to ensure conclusions are robust rather than wishful thinking.  
* Confidence calibration: Overconfident claims erode trust in research and lead to poor decisions by those who rely on the findings. We evaluate whether projects appropriately calibrate their confidence levels and help readers understand how much weight to give different conclusions.

### Scoring rubric

| Score | Research & reasoning transparency | Alternative perspectives | Confidence calibration |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1 | No discussion of challenges or failures; hides key assumptions; ignores obvious limitations; presents work as flawless | No consideration of objections or alternative interpretations; presents findings as definitive | Highly overconfident claims with no hedging; assertions far exceed evidence strength |
| 2-3 | Minimal acknowledgment of challenges; few assumptions stated; limited awareness of constraints | Limited consideration of alternatives; mostly dismisses objections without serious engagement | Overconfident claims; assertions somewhat exceed evidence strength |
| 4-5 | Some discussion of challenges but lacks depth; basic assumptions stated; acknowledges some limitations | Some consideration of alternatives but analysis is superficial | Mostly appropriate confidence but some overconfident claims |
| 6-7 | Good discussion of what didn't work and why; clearly states key assumptions; honest about main limitations | Good consideration of objections and alternatives; engages seriously with competing explanations | Well-calibrated confidence; claims generally match evidence strength |
| 8-9 | Excellent transparency about process and challenges; comprehensive statement of assumptions; honest about all significant limitations | Thoughtful engagement with objections and alternatives; sophisticated analysis of competing interpretations | Expertly calibrated confidence; precisely hedged claims that match evidence |
| 10 | Exceptional transparency and intellectual honesty; treats failures as learning opportunities; complete acknowledgment of limitations and uncertainties | Masterful consideration of alternatives; seeks out strongest objections and engages rigorously | Perfect confidence calibration; demonstrates sophisticated understanding of uncertainty |

## Communication (15%)

### Categories

* Clarity: Even brilliant research becomes useless if stakeholders can't understand it. We evaluate whether projects communicate complex ideas clearly in both their written reports and presentations, making findings accessible without losing important nuance.  
* Structure: Poorly organized research communication confuses audiences and obscures key insights. We evaluate whether projects demonstrate logical flow in their written work and presentations that helps stakeholders follow the reasoning and trust the findings.  
* Engagement: Research that fails to capture attention gets ignored, regardless of content quality. We evaluate whether projects communicate their findings in compelling ways through both writing style and presentation delivery that motivate stakeholders to act on the results.

### Scoring rubric

| Score | Clarity | Structure | Engagement |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1 | Ideas are confusing or incomprehensible; loses nuance or accuracy in explanation | No clear structure; random or illogical flow that hinders understanding | Fails to engage readers or audience; poor writing style and delivery, ineffective organization and time management |
| 2-3 | Basic clarity but some confusing sections; either oversimplifies or remains too technical | Some structure but flow is choppy; occasional logical gaps | Struggles to maintain reader and audience interest; some engagement issues in both writing and presentation, poor time management |
| 4-5 | Generally clear explanation with minor confusing elements; reasonable balance of accessibility and nuance | Adequate structure with mostly logical flow; some organizational issues | Moderately engaging writing and presentation; maintains some reader and audience interest, adequate organization and time management |
| 6-7 | Clear explanation of most concepts; good balance of accessibility and nuance | Good structure with logical flow; builds understanding systematically | Engaging report and presentation; captures and maintains reader and audience interest through clear writing and good delivery, effective time management |
| 8-9 | Excellent clarity; complex ideas made accessible while preserving important nuance | Strong structure; clear logical progression that enhances understanding | Highly engaging written work and presentation; excellent connection with readers and audience, polished writing style and delivery, excellent time management |
| 10 | Perfect clarity; masterful explanation that makes complex ideas accessible to any audience without losing nuance | Flawless structure; optimal flow that maximizes audience understanding | Exceptionally engaging report and presentation; captivates readers and audience throughout, professional writing and presentation skills |

# How the rubric differs from academic norms

This rubric intentionally rewards research practices that may differ from conventional academic norms. Understanding these differences helps explain what we're looking for and why.

# Impact over academic contribution

Traditional academic research often focuses on contributing to scholarly literature, with success measured by citations and theoretical advancement within the field. Our rubric puts more emphasis on research that actually tackles important problems and can tangibly influence real-world decisions, policies, or practices. We want to reward thoughtful prioritization and reasoning about what matters.

# Transparency about process and failures

Academic papers typically present polished results with the messy research process hidden from view. Failed experiments and dead ends rarely make it into publication. Our rubric explicitly rewards honest acknowledgment of what didn't work and treats failures as valuable learning opportunities. We want to see the real research journey, not just the success story.

# Accessible communication over technical sophistication

Academic writing often uses specialized jargon and assumes expert audiences, where dense prose can signal scholarly rigor. Our rubric values making complex ideas accessible to broader audiences without losing important nuance and technical details. This can be difficult in some cases and we don’t want you to compromise on accuracy. However, we encourage you to spend time on communicating your work as clearly as possible instead of using technical jargon for its own sake.

# Strategic focus over comprehensive coverage

Academic research often tries to be exhaustively comprehensive, addressing all relevant literature and potential angles to avoid criticism. This rubric puts more emphasis on strategic focusing and realistic scoping over breadth. Given the time constraints of the fellowship, we'd rather see meaningful progress on carefully chosen aspects of a problem than shallow coverage of everything related to it. 

# Innovation as enhancement, not requirement

Academic culture often pressures researchers to claim novelty and originality as their primary contribution. This is especially challenging for junior researchers like you. Our rubric treats innovation as a valuable bonus. Exceptional execution of proven approaches can score equally highly. Don't feel compelled to reinvent the wheel – sometimes the best research applies existing methods excellently to important problems.

# Explicit discussion of uncertainty 

Academic papers often minimize uncertainty and hedge language to improve their chance at publication. Our rubric rewards appropriately calibrated confidence and nuanced understanding of limitations. Being honest about what you don't know and how confident you should be in different conclusions builds rather than undermines credibility.
